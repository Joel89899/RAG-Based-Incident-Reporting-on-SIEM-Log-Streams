# Scientific & Thesis Writing

Guidance distilled from Chris A. Mack, *How to Write a Good Scientific Paper* (SPIE Press, 2018). Use this as a checklist-driven editor/co-author for thesis chapters, journal papers, and dissertation sections — not just a style guide to recite.

## Core principle

A scientific paper (and a thesis, which is a bundle of the same rhetorical units) does not require literary talent — it requires following a well-established structure so the *science* stays the focus. When reviewing or drafting, check structure and logic first, sentence-level style second.

## 1. Structure: IMRaD

Every research chapter/paper should map onto: **Introduction → Method → Results and Discussion → Conclusions**.

- **Introduction** moves general → specific, in three phases: establish a territory (field, why it matters, prior work), establish a niche (the gap/question/challenge), occupy the niche (state purpose, announce the present work). The introduction must implicitly stake three of the four publishability claims: scope, novelty, significance (quality is demonstrated, not asserted). Avoid: restating the abstract, needless background, exaggerated importance, unclear research question.
- **Method** must give enough detail that an independent researcher in the field could reproduce the work and — just as importantly — *judge its validity* (internal validity: do results support the conclusions; external validity: how far do conclusions generalize). Justify why this method was chosen, not just what it was. Avoid: burying results here, chronological narration, unneeded proprietary/commercial detail.
- **Results and Discussion**: results presented in logical (not chronological) order, clearly distinguishing new vs. previously published results. Discussion must explain the results, not just restate them — typical arc: summarize → expected/unexpected → compare to prior work → interpret via theory/model → discuss generality and limits. Avoid: results never discussed, discussion untethered to results, ignoring inconvenient data, conclusions without argument.
- **Conclusions**: brief summary of results and discussion, implications and key message, the most general claims the evidence actually supports, and a future outlook. Avoid: repeating the abstract or intro, new evidence/arguments not previously presented, unaddressed research questions.

When reviewing a draft, check first whether content is in the *right* section — misplaced results/conclusions is one of the most common structural defects.

## 2. Scientific style (the five stylistic commitments)

The scientific register differs from general nonfiction on five axes — use these to explain *why* a sentence feels off, not just that it does:

- **Truth**: reality is objective and writer-independent; the author has no privileged access, so avoid overclaiming or dramatizing.
- **Presentation**: valued in this order — accuracy, precision, clarity, concision, then grace. Never sacrifice accuracy for elegance. Qualify claims (hedge) rather than overstate; but also don't underclaim.
- **Scene**: imagine presenting to a seminar audience of interested peers — formal, professional, no colloquialisms, but still readable aloud.
- **Cast**: readers are intelligent peers, not necessarily niche experts — don't assume too much or too little.
- **Thought and language**: no idea is inexpressible with the right words; sloppy wording signals sloppy thinking. Precision failures are the writer's fault even when a reader misreads.

Practical consequences: passive voice is often *correct* in scientific writing (removes the replaceable author from the foreground: "an experiment was performed"), except in intro/conclusions where authorial claims of novelty or direction warrant "I/we." Favor complex noun phrases over embedded clauses to avoid ambiguity. Omit needless words: if removing a word doesn't hurt accuracy, precision, or clarity, cut it. Spell out an acronym on first use; avoid acronyms in the abstract unless essential and self-defined there too.

## 3. Abstract

Write the abstract **last**, after the manuscript is finished — otherwise it drifts from the body (studies found ~40% of medical-journal abstracts contained claims inconsistent with or absent from the body).

Use the **structured-abstract method** even for journals that don't require visible structure:
1. Draft one sentence (or two) each for: **Background** (what makes this work necessary/interesting), **Aim** (what gap is being filled), **Approach** (what was actually done), **Results** (main findings, with numbers if relevant), **Conclusions** (why it matters, where it leads).
2. Delete the subheadings, merge into one paragraph, add light transitions only if needed.

Target ~150–200 words. Final abstract checks:
- Every claim in the abstract is consistent with and traceable to the body.
- No citations (unless truly unavoidable, then give the full citation inline).
- No acronyms unless essential (spelled out even there), no figure/table references, no first person, no throat-clearing phrases ("in this paper," "we report," "will be discussed," "novel/new" as a label).
- Self-contained: understandable without having read the paper.

## 4. Title

Written after the abstract. Should be specific enough to be informative on its own, but not so long it becomes unreadable. Reflects aim + approach, not results/conclusions. Avoid: punny or clever titles (hurt searchability and translation), acronyms/jargon/trademarks, titles too short to be descriptive.

## 5. Figures, tables, and data

- Every piece of data ideally has four parts: description, number, unit, uncertainty estimate — put all four in the figure/caption where practical.
- Captions should (1) describe everything in the graph, (2) draw attention to what matters, (3) state the main conclusion to be drawn, when practical.
- Use log scales to *reveal* trends, not obscure them; use ≥80% of the plotted range to avoid wasted white space.
- Tables beat graphs for small datasets or exact lookup values; graphs beat tables for trends/comparisons.
- Avoid: chart titles (put that info in the caption instead), pie charts, unnecessary bar charts, spurious 3D effects, gridline clutter, inconsistent figure formatting across the document.
- Every figure/table must be referred to in the text, first-referenced in numerical order.

## 6. Citations

Five goals of a citation: give context for critical analysis, give background so the target audience can follow, present alternative/contrary evidence rather than hiding it, credit prior work relied upon, and stay current (cite the latest relevant work, not just convenient older work). Verify every reference yourself — citation accuracy is the author's responsibility, not the reviewer's. Avoid spurious citations, biased selection (omitting inconvenient prior work), and excessive self-citation.

## 7. Pre-submission / defense-readiness checklist

Before treating a chapter or paper as done, or before a thesis defense, run this checklist (based on the reviewer/editor checklist in the source):

**Reject-level questions** (any "no" here is a stop-ship problem):
- Does content match the venue's scope?
- Are the results genuinely novel (or, for a thesis, is the contribution clearly separated from prior work)?
- Are results significant enough to be worth the reader's time?
- Do the data actually support the conclusions?
- Is the writing itself clear enough to let a reader evaluate the above?

**Section-by-section pass**: re-run the structural checks in Section 1 above per chapter (Introduction / Method / Results & Discussion / Conclusions), then the Section 3 abstract checks, then:
- Acronyms: spelled out on first use in the body; title/abstract acronym rules followed.
- Figures/tables: captioned, referenced in order, no missing error bars/uncertainty where relevant.
- Citations: none spurious, none missing for load-bearing claims, self-citations proportionate.

## How to apply this when asked to "review my thesis" or similar

1. Ask (or infer from context) which chapter/section is in scope if the whole thesis wasn't provided.
2. Read the actual text before commenting — don't give generic advice divorced from what's on the page.
3. Structure first: is content in the right IMRaD section? Does the intro stake scope/novelty/significance? Does discussion explain rather than restate results?
4. Style second: passive vs. active misuse, hedge/overclaim balance, needless words, ambiguous noun phrases.
5. Abstract/title last, and only meaningfully reviewable once the body is stable — flag if abstract was evidently drafted before the current body text.
6. Give concrete, quoted (paraphrased per copyright rules) examples from their draft rather than only abstract principles.
