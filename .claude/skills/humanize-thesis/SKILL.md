name: humanize-thesis
description: A working style guide for producing and revising thesis text that reads ashuman-authored, argued rather than summarized, specific rather than general, and structurally irregular rather than templated.
---

Use it in two modes,
- **Generation** — apply Parts 0–5 while drafting.
- **Review** — run Part 7 against any draft (yours or AI-assisted).

---

## Part 0 — What actually reads as "AI" (read this first)

Surface vocabulary is the smallest problem. The deeper tells are structural and
argumentative. A draft can contain zero banned words and still read as machine
output if it does any of the following:

- Makes broad claims that no specific source is on the hook for ("research shows,"
  "studies suggest," "it is widely accepted").
- Hedges everything to the same degree, so nothing is asserted and nothing risked.
- Balances every point with a symmetric counterpoint ("while X offers benefits,
  it also presents challenges").
- Writes paragraphs that state a claim, list support, then restate the claim.
- Surveys a field instead of taking a position within it.
- Sounds equally confident about the trivial and the contested.

Fix these and the vocabulary problem mostly takes care of itself. Fix only the
vocabulary and the text still reads as a competent summary rather than a thesis.

## Part 1 — Vocabulary: red flags, not a blocklist

Treat the words below as signals to stop and check, not as forbidden tokens. The
goal is precise language, not thesaurus substitution. Swapping "leverage" for
"harness" produces a different tell, not human prose.

**Almost never justified — replace or cut:**
utilize (use "use"), delve into, showcase, underscore, tapestry, landscape
(metaphorical), testament to, cornerstone, paradigm shift, navigate
(metaphorical), streamline, foster, cultivate, in the realm/world of, serves as,
stands as, plays a [crucial/key/vital] role in, rich history/tradition.

**Allowed only when earned by evidence in the same passage:**
crucial, pivotal, significant, key, robust, essential. If you can't state *why*
and cite the basis, cut the word instead of softening it.

**Current constructions that flag AI, not on most older lists:**
- Em-dash used for a dramatic reveal. Keep at most one per page; the rest are
  usually commas, colons, or full stops.
- "Not just X, but Y" / "It's not X; it's Y."
- Correlative range padding: "from X to Y to Z."
- "It's worth noting that" / "It is important to note that."
- Empty tricolons (see Part 2).
- Participle tails that add fake depth: "...thereby contributing to a broader
  understanding of the field."
- "Increasingly," "in today's world," "in an era of," "now more than ever."
- Vague attribution: "experts agree," "many scholars," "research suggests" with
  no citation attached.

**Banned transitions** (as in your original — these are safe absolute cuts):
Furthermore, Moreover, Additionally, In conclusion, Ultimately, It is important
to note that, As we can see. Prefer no connective, or a specific logical one
(because, however, by contrast, if so, since).

## Part 2 — Sentence-level structure

- **Break the rule of three.** AI groups adjectives, examples, and clauses in
  threes. Use one exact descriptor, or split the ideas across sentences. A
  tricolon should be rare and deliberate, never the default cadence.
- **Vary length — irregularly.** Mix long and short, but do not fall into a
  fixed long-then-short rhythm. A metronomic pattern is itself a humanizer tell.
  Some paragraphs can run several medium sentences with no short one at all.
- **No rhetorical questions.** State the problem directly. Not "But what does
  this mean for policy?" — instead, "This result constrains policy in two ways."
- **Active voice where your discipline allows it** (see Part 5). "The data show"
  beats "It is shown by the data" in the humanities and much social science.
  In many experimental sciences, impersonal or passive construction is the
  required register — follow the field, not a blanket rule.

---

## Part 3 — Paragraph structure

- Kill the topic-sentence-then-restatement shape. Let evidence or a specific
  observation open the paragraph; let the claim emerge from it.
- One analytical job per paragraph. If a paragraph both introduces a concept and
  applies it, split it or cut the weaker half.
- Allow ideas to bridge across the paragraph break without a tidy wrap-up. Not
  every paragraph needs a closing summary sentence.
- Vary how paragraphs open. If three in a row start with the subject of the
  section ("The model...," "The model also...," "The model further..."), rework
  two of them.

## Part 4 — Argumentation (the part that matters most for a thesis)

- **Make claims that could be wrong.** A thesis takes a position a competent
  reader could dispute. If every sentence is unobjectionable, there's no thesis.
- **Attach every empirical claim to a specific source.** Replace "studies show X"
  with "Author (Year) found X in [population/condition]." If you can't name the
  source, you can't make the claim.
- **Calibrate hedging.** Hedge where the evidence is genuinely uncertain; state
  plainly where it isn't. Uniform hedging reads as evasive and machine-like.
  "may potentially suggest that it could" → "suggests."
- **Engage counterarguments by name,** not with a symmetric "on the other hand."
  Say which scholar or result cuts against you and why your reading survives it.
- **State stakes concretely.** Not "this has important implications," but the
  specific thing that changes if you're right — a method that becomes usable, a
  prior finding that no longer holds, a measurement now possible.
- **Prefer specifics to categories.** "Various factors" → name them. "Recent
  work" → cite it. Numbers, mechanisms, dates, and named cases over abstractions.

## Part 5 — Discipline calibration (do this once, up front)

Conventions differ enough that a single "academic voice" is wrong for most
theses. Before drafting, pull 3–5 recent papers from your exact subfield and
match:

- **Person and voice.** First person and active voice are normal in history,
  philosophy, and parts of the social sciences; impersonal/passive is expected
  in most experimental and clinical writing.
- **Hedging norms.** Hedged and cautious in medicine and empirical psychology;
  more assertive in mathematics and theoretical work.
- **Citation density.** A humanities paragraph may carry one citation; a
  biomedical one may carry six.
- **Terminology.** Use the field's exact terms of art, not general paraphrases
  a non-specialist would choose.

If the rewrite ignores your field's register, it will read as generic academic
mush regardless of vocabulary.

## Part 6 — Generation protocol

1. Write the claim first, in one blunt sentence. Then find and cite its support.
   If no support exists, weaken or drop the claim before writing the paragraph.
2. Draft ugly. Do not self-edit for elegance on the first pass; get the argument
   down, then cut.
3. Keep one idea per paragraph. When a second idea appears, start a new one.
4. Prefer the plain word. Reach for a fancier one only when it is more precise,
   never when it is merely more impressive.
5. Leave deliberate asymmetry: a paragraph that ends mid-thought, a short section,
   an unhedged assertion where you're sure. Human writing is uneven on purpose.

## Part 7 — Review / self-audit checklist

Run these against any draft. Each is a search-and-fix, not a vibe check.

**Vocabulary**
- [ ] Search the document for every word in Part 1's "almost never" list. Replace
      or cut each hit.
- [ ] Count em-dashes. More than roughly one per page → convert most.
- [ ] Find every tricolon (three items in a row). Break all but the deliberate ones.

**Argument**
- [ ] Find every generalization. Does a specific citation sit next to it? If not,
      cite or cut.
- [ ] Find every "research/studies/experts show." Name the source or delete the
      sentence.
- [ ] Find stacked hedges ("may possibly suggest"). Reduce to one hedge or none.
- [ ] Is there at least one claim a reader could reasonably dispute? If not, the
      draft has no thesis yet.
- [ ] For each counterargument, is a specific opposing source or result named?

**Structure**
- [ ] Read a page aloud. Does the rhythm feel metronomic (long-short-long-short)?
      Rework until it's irregular.
- [ ] Does any paragraph state a claim, list support, then restate the claim?
      Cut the restatement and, if needed, the opening claim.
- [ ] Do several consecutive paragraphs open the same way? Vary them.

**Register**
- [ ] Does person, voice, hedging, and citation density match the sample papers
      from your subfield (Part 5)?

**Final pass**
- [ ] If the text feels perfectly balanced, faintly impressed with its own
      subject, or free of any contestable claim, it is still reading as AI.
      Rewrite the weakest section by hand.
